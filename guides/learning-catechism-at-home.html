Learning a Catechism at Home: A Simple Place to Begin

What catechisms teach, how to choose one, and how to make question-and-answer learning part of family devotion.

A catechism is a collection of questions and answers designed to teach the Christian faith.

For generations, Christians have used catechisms to help children and adults learn what Scripture teaches about God, creation, sin, salvation, prayer, and the Christian life.

The method is straightforward. One person asks a question. Another responds. Over time, the words become familiar, opening opportunities for explanation, conversation, and reflection.

Catechism learning does not need to be complicated, and it does not need to become a separate lesson that fills an entire evening.

One question, thoughtfully considered and returned to, is a good place to begin.

What is the purpose of a catechism?

Catechisms help organize Christian teaching into memorable questions and answers.

They are useful because they provide a framework for subjects that might otherwise be approached only occasionally or without a clear order.

A question about God may lead to a question about creation. Questions about sin may lead to the person and work of Jesus Christ. Questions about prayer may open a discussion of how believers depend upon God.

But catechisms have an important limitation: they are not Scripture.

Their answers should be understood and tested in light of God’s Word. Their value lies in helping us understand and remember biblical teaching, not in replacing the Bible with another authority.

The goal is not merely to memorize correct sentences. It is to understand what those sentences mean and why they matter.

Which catechism should you use?

There is no single catechism that every Christian household must use.

Different catechisms arose within different Christian traditions and were written for different instructional purposes.

Redevotion’s Library includes four historic or historically rooted resources.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism

The Westminster Shorter Catechism, originally published as The Shorter Catechism, was completed by the Westminster Assembly in 1647.

It contains 107 questions and answers and presents a systematic account of Christian doctrine and duty within the Westminster Presbyterian tradition.

Its questions move through foundational teachings about God and humanity, God’s commandments, salvation, the means of grace, and prayer.

It can provide a sustained structure for households or individuals who want to work through Christian doctrine in an organized way.

The Baptist Catechism

The Baptist Catechism, also historically titled A Brief Instruction in the Principles of Christian Religion, is associated with the English Particular Baptist tradition.

The earliest known surviving edition is the fifth edition, printed in London in 1695. It contains 114 questions and answers.

The Baptist Catechism grew from the same tradition as the Westminster Shorter Catechism and follows it closely: much of its order is the same, and many of its answers are word for word alike. The clearest differences concern baptism. The Westminster Shorter Catechism calls baptism and the Lord’s Supper sacraments and, in keeping with its understanding of the covenant of grace, teaches that baptism is for those who profess faith and also for the infants of church members. The Baptist Catechism calls baptism and the Lord’s Supper ordinances and teaches that baptism is for those who personally profess repentance and faith in Christ, administered by immersion. Its additional questions include more on baptism and on joining a local church.

These are not the only points where the two traditions differ, and the catechisms are not interchangeable on them. Families should choose a catechism with an understanding of their own church’s teaching and confessional commitments.

Catechism for Young Children

Catechism for Young Children, subtitled Being an Introduction to the Shorter Catechism, was published in 1840 by the Presbyterian Board of Publication. Redevotion’s source text comes from a later Board printing.

It contains 145 questions. They are generally brief and provide an accessible introduction to foundational Christian teaching.

Although its historical title identifies children as the intended audience, there is no universal age at which every child should begin or master particular questions.

The better guide is the learner’s ability to listen, understand, remember, and discuss what is being taught.

A Catechism for Boys and Girls

A Catechism for Boys and Girls is included in Redevotion using the Chapel Library edition, which carries a 1998 copyright notice. It contains 134 questions.

It offers another question-and-answer approach to foundational Christian instruction.

Unlike the other three resources, Redevotion presents this catechism in the wording supplied by its publisher, rather than offering a separately modernized reading.

Its wording should be respected as a distinct published resource.

Modern and Traditional readings: what’s the difference?

Some older catechisms use expressions that are no longer common in everyday English.

Traditional wording preserves that older language. A Modern reading makes selected expressions more immediately understandable while aiming to preserve the intended meaning.

In Redevotion, Modern and Traditional readings are available for:

  • The Westminster Shorter Catechism
  • The Baptist Catechism
  • The Catechism for Young Children (a Traditional reading is given where its older wording differs)

A Catechism for Boys and Girls is presented in the publisher’s wording only.

Neither option should be treated as inherently better for every learner.

Some households value learning familiar historic expressions. Others find that modern wording helps them explain the answer more clearly.

A useful principle is to choose the wording you can teach faithfully and understand together.

How to begin catechism learning at home

The simplest approach is often the most sustainable.

1. Begin with one question

Choose a catechism that fits your church’s teaching and your household’s needs.

Read the first question aloud. Let the learner think about it before giving the answer.

There is no need to begin with several questions or set an ambitious completion schedule.

2. Read the answer and explain it

After hearing the response, read the catechism’s answer together.

If a word is unfamiliar, explain it in ordinary language. Ask what the answer means rather than focusing only on whether every word was recited correctly.

This is especially valuable when learning older theological vocabulary.

3. Connect the answer to Scripture

A catechism provides a summary. Scripture provides the foundation.

Many catechisms include Scripture proofs: references to biblical passages associated with their answers.

Look up one or two references where they are provided. Read the surrounding passage when helpful and ask how it relates to the question.

Not every catechism includes these references in Redevotion. The Westminster Shorter Catechism, Baptist Catechism, and A Catechism for Boys and Girls have Scripture proofs for nearly every question.

Catechism for Young Children does not include a Scripture-proof list in Redevotion.

You can still open the Bible together to explore the teaching.

4. Return to the same question

Repetition can be helpful, but it does not require turning learning into a performance.

Return to a question over several gatherings. Recite the answer together, then ask the learner to explain it in their own words.

When the question has become familiar, introduce another.

There is no need to insist on perfect recall before every conversation can move forward. Understanding and memorization can develop together.

5. Make room for questions

Children and adults alike may have questions about an answer.

Those questions are not necessarily interruptions to catechism learning. They are often where the most useful instruction begins.

Sometimes the right response is to explain the matter simply. Sometimes it is to read more Scripture, consult a trusted resource, or ask a pastor or teacher for help.

Catechism learning should encourage thoughtful engagement with Christian doctrine, not discourage honest questions.

What if the children in your household are different ages?

A household does not need every member to work through the same material in the same way.

You might use a brief children’s catechism with a younger learner while an older child or adult studies the Westminster Shorter or Baptist Catechism more deeply.

Alternatively, everyone can consider the same question, with different levels of explanation.

A younger child may practice a short answer. An older child might explain the terms or read a supporting Scripture passage. Adults can participate rather than merely supervise.

The point is shared instruction at a level each person can follow, not dividing the household into competing curricula.

Bringing catechism into family worship

Catechism learning can fit naturally within a larger gathering around Scripture and prayer.

A family might:

  1. Read a passage of Scripture.
  2. Ask one catechism question.
  3. Read or recite its answer.
  4. Discuss a related verse or biblical truth.
  5. Pray together.

On some days, the catechism portion may take only a short time. On others, a question may lead to a longer conversation.

A brief gathering does not need to feel incomplete.

And if your household does not currently gather for family worship, catechism learning can begin on its own—during a quiet moment, over a meal, or whenever there is a natural opportunity to talk about Scripture.

Using Redevotion for catechism learning

Redevotion is being developed to provide a quiet, organized place for practices like Scripture reading, catechism learning, prayer, and family worship.

Its Catechism practice presents questions in order, allows you to work through a few at a time, and keeps answers hidden until you choose to reveal them.

You can explore the supported reading options and available Scripture proofs in How Redevotion works: Catechism, or see how catechism and Scripture memory can fit together in Ways to Use Redevotion.

Whatever resource you choose, begin with the purpose behind it: to know God’s Word more deeply, understand the faith more clearly, and speak of these truths together.
