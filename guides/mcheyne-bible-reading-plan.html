The M’Cheyne Bible Reading Plan, Explained

How a historic Bible reading calendar can bring Scripture into personal devotion and family worship.

Reading the Bible regularly can be simple in principle and difficult in practice. Where should you begin? What should you read next? How can a household read Scripture together while each person also has time for private devotion?

In 1842, Scottish pastor Robert Murray M’Cheyne prepared a Bible reading calendar for his congregation in Dundee. His aim was to help believers read through Scripture in an orderly way and share in the same portions of God’s Word.

The plan he developed remains widely used today. Its distinctive feature is not merely the number of chapters it assigns, but the way it brings together family worship and personal reading.

How the M’Cheyne plan works

The calendar assigns four Scripture readings each day, arranged in parallel tracks rather than taking the Bible straight through from Genesis to Revelation.

Across a year, the plan reads the Old Testament books other than Psalms once, and the New Testament and Psalms twice.

The readings ordinarily come from different parts of Scripture, allowing the reader to move through biblical history, the Gospels, the Psalms, and the rest of the Bible alongside one another.

For example, the first day’s readings are Genesis 1, Matthew 1, Ezra 1, and Acts 1. Rather than remaining in one biblical book for the entire day’s reading, the calendar opens several passages of Scripture.

This variety is one reason people continue returning to the plan. It provides a clear structure while allowing different portions of God’s Word to inform one another.

What do “Family” and “Secret” mean?

M’Cheyne’s original calendar set aside two readings for family worship and two for what he called secret reading. In the calendar itself, the two pairs of columns are headed Family and Secret.

In the language of his day, secret reading meant private devotion—time spent personally reading God’s Word and drawing near to Him in prayer. It did not mean that the passages were hidden or restricted.

The distinction served a practical purpose.

Family readings provided Scripture to read and discuss together. M’Cheyne encouraged those leading family worship to consider the passage beforehand and ask simple questions that would help the household understand it.

Secret readings provided Scripture for personal attention, meditation, and prayer.

The original directions were not inflexible. M’Cheyne acknowledged that a passage assigned to family worship might sometimes be more suitable for private reading, and vice versa.

In Redevotion, the two groups are called Family and Private. M’Cheyne himself, in his directions, speaks of a family chapter being “read in private.” The newer term keeps the same distinction while making its meaning more immediately understandable.

What if you don’t have a family gathering?

The plan can still be used entirely for personal Bible reading.

You might read the four passages in one sitting, divide them between morning and evening, or approach them in smaller portions throughout the day.

The Family and Private headings describe the calendar’s original design. They do not require you to live in a particular type of household or conduct family worship in order to benefit from the readings.

For a household that does gather for worship, however, the division offers a useful opportunity: everyone can share part of the same reading plan without making every passage a group activity.

How to keep a reading plan without making it a burden

A calendar can remove the daily decision of what to read. But it can also become discouraging when the schedule begins to feel more important than the Scripture itself.

M’Cheyne recognized this danger.

In his original introduction, he warned against formalism, self-righteousness, careless reading, and turning the calendar into a burden. “My desire,” he wrote, “is not to cast a snare upon you, but to be a helper of your joy.”

That distinction still matters.

Begin with the passage, not the checklist

Before reading, take a moment to ask God for understanding.

Read attentively. Notice what the passage teaches about God’s character, His works, His promises, and His commands.

A reading plan tells you where to turn. It cannot replace the attention and prayer with which you read.

When you fall behind, begin again

Missed readings need not become a source of guilt.

Some readers choose to catch up gradually. Others continue from the next available portion or return to the point where they stopped.

There is no need to turn a missed day into a week of hurried reading merely to make the calendar appear complete.

A shorter, attentive reading is more valuable than rushing through several chapters without hearing what they say.

Let Scripture lead into prayer

The plan becomes more useful when reading is not treated as an isolated task.

After a passage, consider a simple question: What has this shown me about God, and how should I respond?

You might give thanks, confess sin, bring a need before the Lord, or pray for someone else.

This does not require a lengthy additional routine. It is simply a way of allowing Scripture to shape your prayers.

How does M’Cheyne compare with other reading plans?

A reading plan is a tool for approaching Scripture, not a measure of spiritual maturity.

A whole-Bible-in-a-year plan provides broad exposure to the entire biblical canon within a defined period. Different plans may follow a sequential or parallel order.

A New Testament plan offers a narrower reading scope for someone who wants to spend sustained time with the Gospels, Acts, the Epistles, and Revelation.

A Psalms-and-Proverbs plan concentrates on biblical poetry, prayer, praise, and wisdom.

The M’Cheyne plan is distinctive in combining broad annual reading with parallel passages and an intentional division between household and private devotion.

The most useful plan is one that helps you continue reading Scripture thoughtfully, with a rhythm appropriate to your circumstances.

Using M’Cheyne for personal and family devotion

One of the most helpful aspects of M’Cheyne’s arrangement is that personal and family readings belong to one coordinated calendar.

A household can gather around the Family passages while individuals continue with the Private passages separately.

That connection makes the reading plan more than a list of chapters. It gives a family a shared place in Scripture while leaving room for personal attention to God’s Word.

Redevotion is being developed to support this approach. Its M’Cheyne plan preserves the distinction between Family and Private readings and allows the Family readings to be used within Family Worship.

You can learn more about this approach in How Redevotion works: Bible Reading.

The purpose remains the same as it was in 1842: not simply to finish a calendar, but to return regularly to Scripture with attention, prayer, and delight.
